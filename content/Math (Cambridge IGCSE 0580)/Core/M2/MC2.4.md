# MC2.4 Indices II | Save My Exams Revision Notes

## 1. Core Intuition & Mental Model ("What is it?")
Indices (also known as powers or exponents) are shorthand notation for repeated multiplication. An expression like $x^4$ means multiplying $x$ by itself $4$ times: $x \times x \times x \times x$. The index laws provide short cuts to multiply, divide, or raise powered terms to further powers without writing out repeated multiplications.

Think of index laws as counting rules for exponents. When multiplying terms with matching bases, you combine their counts by adding exponents. When dividing, you cancel shared factors by subtracting exponents. A power of zero represents a state where all base factors cancel completely, leaving $1$.

## 2. Key Definitions, Principles & Formulas
- **Multiplication Law**: Add exponents when multiplying terms with the same base:
  $$x^a \times x^b = x^{a + b}$$
- **Division Law**: Subtract exponents when dividing terms with the same base:
  $$\frac{x^a}{x^b} = x^{a - b}$$
- **Power of a Power Law**: Multiply exponents when raising a powered term to another power:
  $$(x^a)^b = x^{a \times b}$$
- **Zero Index Law**: Any non-zero base raised to the power of zero equals $1$:
  $$x^0 = 1 \quad (x \neq 0)$$
- **Negative Index Law**: A negative index indicates a reciprocal (moving terms between numerator and denominator):
  $$x^{-n} = \frac{1}{x^n}$$

> 💡 **Exam Tip**: Apply index rules separately to numerical coefficients and variable powers. For example, in $(3x^4)^2$, square the $3$ to get $9$, and multiply index $4$ by $2$ to get $x^8$, giving $9x^8$.

> ⚠️ **Common Misconception**: Students often confuse $3x^0$ with $(3x)^0$. In $3x^0$, only $x$ is raised to power $0$, giving $3(1) = 3$. In $(3x)^0$, the entire base $3x$ is raised to power $0$, giving $1$.

## 3. Step-by-Step Method & Worked Exam Demonstration

### Worked Example 1: Simplifying Expressions using Index Laws
(a) Simplify fully: $4x^3 y^2 \times 3x^5 y^4$.
(b) Simplify fully: $\frac{20a^7 b^3}{5a^2 b^3}$.
(c) Evaluate: $5^{-2} + 8^0$.

#### Solution:
(a)
- **Step 1: Multiply numerical coefficients together.**
  $$4 \times 3 = 12$$
- **Step 2: Apply the multiplication law ($x^a \times x^b = x^{a+b}$) to $x$ powers.**
  $$x^3 \times x^5 = x^{3 + 5} = x^8$$
- **Step 3: Apply the multiplication law to $y$ powers.**
  $$y^2 \times y^4 = y^{2 + 4} = y^6$$
- **Step 4: Combine coefficients and variable powers.**
  $$12x^8 y^6$$

(b)
- **Step 1: Divide numerical coefficients.**
  $$\frac{20}{5} = 4$$
- **Step 2: Apply division law ($\frac{x^a}{x^b} = x^{a-b}$) to variable $a$.**
  $$\frac{a^7}{a^2} = a^{7 - 2} = a^5$$
- **Step 3: Apply division law to variable $b$.**
  $$\frac{b^3}{b^3} = b^{3 - 3} = b^0 = 1$$
- **Step 4: Combine all simplified components.**
  $$4 \times a^5 \times 1 = 4a^5$$

(c)
- **Step 1: Rewrite $5^{-2}$ using negative index reciprocal rule.**
  $$5^{-2} = \frac{1}{5^2} = \frac{1}{25}$$
- **Step 2: Evaluate $8^0$ using the zero index law.**
  $$8^0 = 1$$
- **Step 3: Add fractions together.**
  $$\frac{1}{25} + 1 = 1\frac{1}{25} \text{ or } \frac{26}{25} \text{ or } 1.04$$

## 4. Common Misconceptions & Diagnostic Exam Pitfalls
- **Multiplying Coefficients and Indices**: Writing $x^3 \times x^4 = x^{12}$ instead of $x^{3+4} = x^7$. Exponents are added, not multiplied.
- **Forgetting Coefficient Powers in Bracket Expansion**: Expanding $(2x^3)^4$ as $2x^{12}$ instead of $2^4 x^{12} = 16x^{12}$.
- **Negative Sign Confusion**: Thinking $x^{-2}$ is a negative number. $x^{-2} = \frac{1}{x^2}$, which is positive for all real $x \neq 0$.
